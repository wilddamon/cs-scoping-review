"After the consolidation of centralised control of the Mediterranean Basin and increasingly vast swathes of inland Europe and Asia with the rise of Augustus as Princeps, Rome's empire began to be viewed not as a local or regional, but global hegemony. In what has often been considered something of a poetic state manifesto for this new Roman Imperialism, Virgil's Aeneid sees Jupiter ordain for the Romans an empire without end ("imperium sine fine" Aen. 1.279), without limit in space or time. The Caesarian and Claudian Invasions of Britain in 55 BC and AD 43, as well as subsequent campaigns of consolidation on the island, had pushed the scope of Roman power into realms perceived as the outer Ocean of the world. And yet, there were lands even beyond this: another large island variously thought to lie to the west or north of Britain and a country of the farflung furthest north bounded by hard, frozen seas. Was it within Rome's destiny to bring the inhabitants of these lands, if indeed there were any, under their imperium? This article seeks to examine perceptions of Ireland and the enigmatic land of Thule as boundaries of the populated world in the culture of the Roman Empire in the first and second centuries AD and how their conquest, whether real or imagined, reflected the goals, successes, and potential failures of imperialism.
