"OBJECTIVE: In the United States, 9.2% of maternal deaths are attributable to postpartum hemorrhage (PPH).1 Methods of measuring qualitative blood loss (QBL) have become standard of care and widely adopted. However, previous studies that investigated the clinical use thereof have been mixed.2e4 Following the implementation of a QBL-based obstetrical hemorrhage assessment protocol, we aimed to evaluate the relationship between QBL protocol adoption and time to recognition of clinically significant blood loss as defined by ordering a blood transfusion. STUDY DESIGN: This retrospective cohort study was conducted using electronic medical records data that were collected from an urban tertiary referral delivery hospital. Before implementation of the QBL protocol, an estimated blood loss (EBL) method was employed based on visual estimates alone. The QBL protocol that was adopted was a hybrid system that used volumetric obstetrical drapes, graduated suction cannisters, and the weight of blood-stained materials. Deliveries between December 2013 to December 2019 were divided into before- and after-implementation cohorts with exclusion of a washout period in 2017 during which the QBL protocol was implemented and after which QBL quantification was universally used. Relevant demographic and clinical data were extracted from each patient's chart. We defined and calculated PPH as blood loss >1000 mL for all patients included in the analysis. Before 2017, delivering clinicians used the historic 500 mL threshold for vaginal delivery. Transfusion was defined as the administration of packed red blood cells within 24 hours of delivery. Transfusion was at the discretion of the delivering clinician. Time point 0 was delivery of the fetal head. The transfuse order timestamp was used as a proxy for the recognition of clinically meaningful hemorrhage. Medical and surgical interventions for bleeding were at the discretion of the delivering obstetrician. No new medications or devices were introduced during the study period. Characteristics between the groups were compared using Student's t tests and chi-square tests when appropriate. The primary outcome was hours from delivery to transfusion, which was calculated using a multivariable Cox regression to obtain an adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) and plotted as Kaplan- Meier survival curves. The mode of delivery and payor status were selected a priori for adjustment. Secondary outcomes were blood loss, PPH frequency, transfusion frequency, and number of packed red blood cell units transfused. Categorical outcomes were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).
