"Background: Neonatal surgical emergencies pose a critical challenge in pediatric care, often requiring immediate intervention to improve survival outcomes. Identifying perinatal risk factors during antenatal care offers a valuable opportunity to optimize early detection, timely referral, and comprehensive neonatal management. Aim(s): To evaluate the spectrum of neonatal surgical emergency admissions and analyze perinatal risk factors identifiable during antenatal care that influence outcomes and hospital resource utilization. Material(s) and Method(s): This prospective observational study was conducted over a period of 24 months in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) and Department of Pediatric Surgery at a tertiary care referral hospital. Eighty neonates aged 0-28 days admitted with surgical emergencies were enrolled. Maternal data included demographics, consanguinity, comorbidities, antenatal visits, and ultrasound findings. Neonatal parameters included birth weight, gestational age, mode of delivery, APGAR scores, and timing of presentation. Diagnoses, surgical management, and outcomes were documented. Result(s): The mean birth weight and gestational age were 2650 +/- 450 g and 36.8 +/- 2.4 weeks, respectively. Male neonates predominated (60%). Common surgical conditions included tracheoesophageal fistula (22.5%), anorectal malformations (20.0%), and intestinal atresias (17.5%). Antenatal ultrasound abnormalities were identified in 67.5% of cases, with highest diagnostic accuracy for neural tube defects (91.7%). Postoperative morbidity and mortality rates were 28.8% and 13.8%, respectively. Multiple regression revealed that fewer than four antenatal visits (p=0.007), consanguinity (p=0.018), maternal hypertension (p=0.011), and abnormal antenatal USG findings (p=0.010) significantly predicted prolonged NICU stay (R2 = 0.422). Conclusion(s): Neonatal surgical emergencies represent a significant burden, often associated with identifiable perinatal risk factors. Abnormal antenatal ultrasonography, inadequate prenatal care, maternal hypertension, and consanguinity are significant predictors of adverse outcomes and extended hospital stays.
