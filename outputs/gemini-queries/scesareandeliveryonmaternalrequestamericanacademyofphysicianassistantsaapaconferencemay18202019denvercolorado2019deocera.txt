"PURPOSE During our clinical practice, we noted that many women were opting to deliver via cesarean section, despite lack of medical indications. Data showed that the rate of cesarean sections in New York State has soared in recent years, with a current rate of 34.4%. Although cesarean sections have always been useful forms of surgical interventions for difficult deliveries, they are still surgical procedures and are associated with risks to both mother and infant. This study sought to determine if there were differences in outcomes to primary infants born to healthy mothers based on the mother's choice of mode of delivery. Specific maternal outcomes also were analyzed. Some of the newborn outcomes that were reviewed and analyzed were APGAR scores at 1 and 5 minutes; respiratory distress; need for supplemental oxygen, mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP); admission to the neonatal ICU (NICU); presence of sepsis; length of stay in hospital; and length of stay in NICU. Maternal outcomes also were documented, and included estimated blood loss, units of blood products transfused, maternal length of stay, maternal fever, vaginal lacerations, episiotomies, and injury to bowel or bladder. METHODS This was a retrospective study based on charts from January 2012 to January 2019 at Richmond University Medical Center on Staten Island, N.Y. The charts were divided into two groups based on mode of delivery--trial of labor (TOL) versus cesarean delivery on maternal request (CDMR). Data were collected with respect to previously listed variables. Data were analyzed with descriptive statistics, i-tests, ANOVA, and regression analysis. Institutional review board approval was obtained before data collection. RESULTS Maternal outcomes Difference in length of hospital stay was found to be statistically significant (P < .001), with mothers in the TOL group having an average hospital length of stay of 4.58 days compared with 3.8 days for mothers in the CDMR group. Episiotomy, laceration, and maternal fever were found to be statistically significant in the TOL group (P < .001). Episiotomy and laceration were recordings specific only to mothers in the TOL group. Just under 16% of mothers in the TOL had fever, compared with 0.9% in the CDMR group. Primary newborn outcomes Statistical significance was observed with differences in APGAR scores and length of stay in the NICU. APGAR scores were measured with a median value and associated range of 1 to 10. A wider range of scores was recorded in newborns in the TOL group at 1 minute of life (P = .009). NICU length of stay was statistically significant (P = .006), with newborns in the CDMR group staying longer on average (2.52 days) compared with those in the TOL group at 1.18 days. No statistical significance was found in rate of NICU admissions, supplemental oxygen use, CPAP use, hypoglycemia, ventilator use, or shoulder dystocia between newborns in the two groups. CONCLUSIONS With respect to newborn outcomes, TOL appeared to be the preferred mode of delivery compared with CDMR, due to the longer length of stay in the NICU for newborns born via cesarean delivery. However, with respect to maternal outcomes, CDMR had decreased rates of maternal fever and maternal length of stay but an increased estimated blood loss. Additionally, rates of episiotomy and vaginal laceration were increased in mothers in the TOL group, as those outcomes are exclusive to that mode of delivery. Our data proved our hypothesis to be null, as the risk-benefit ratio in both maternal and neonatal outcomes were equal. Our results have added valuable statistical information to the discussion of elec-tively selecting a cesarean section with no medical need versus undergoing a TOL. At this time, we support the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists' recommendation that the available information does not provide bias for a recommendation for either mode of delivery."""
