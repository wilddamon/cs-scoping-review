"Access to cesarean delivery is an essential procedure for population-level obstetrical care. However, excessively high levels of cesarean often represent non-medically-indicated use, which may result in adverse events and exceed the risk of vaginal deliveries. A recent study found that, at the global level, the cesarean rate has increased from 6.7% in 1990 to 19.7% in 2014. There are significant disparities in cesarean rates both within and between countries. Studying these inequalities can help identify inequity in cesarean delivery rates and inform equity-based interventions. This secondary analysis of demographic and health surveys aimed to analyze cesarean delivery rateswithin a large cohort of countries and monitor changes in inequality over time. Data were initially gathered from women aged 15 to 49 in low- A nd middle-income countries through either demographic and health surveys or multiple indicator cluster surveys. Data were collected for countries running surveys between 2010 and 2014, and between 2000 and 2004 for temporal comparison. Economic status of participants was determined using country-specific wealth indices constructed using principal component analysis and used to create tiers of wealth ranging from poorest-fifth to richest-fifth. Countries were selected based on data availability. Data were disaggregated by asset-based household wealth status and classified into 5 subgroups based on tiers of wealth. Difference and ratio measures were used to report absolute and relative inequalities in each study country, whereas pace of inequality change between subgroups was calculated using a measure of excess change. A total of 72 countries were selected with adequate survey data between 2010 and 2014, whereas 28 of the countries had comparable data between 2000 and 2004. The rates of cesarean delivery were highest (>33%) in Argentina, Columbia, the Dominican Republic, and Egypt, whereas the rates were lowest (<2%) in Chad, Ethiopia, Niger, and South Sudan.When examining rates between countries, the median cesarean delivery rate in the poorest quintile was 3.7%, whereas the rate in the richest quintile was 18.4%. When examining rates within countries, in 21 countries, the cesarean rate was below 10% in the poorest quintile, potentially indicating underuse, whereas above 15%in the richest quintile, indicating overuse. Of all 72 study countries, 18 reported a greater than 20% difference in cesarean delivery rate between the richest and poorest quintiles. Of the 28 study countrieswhere data were available from surveys in 2000 to 2004 and 2010 to 2014, 26 reported increases in national cesarean delivery rates. Cesarean rates tended to increase faster in the richest quintile than the poorest quintile, with a median of 0.9 and 0.2 percentage points per year, respectively. Although most countries' rates of cesarean delivery are increasing, the data show an unbalanced increase with the richest subgroups increasing the fastest. This trend represents increasing wealth-related inequality in access to this essential obstetrical service.
