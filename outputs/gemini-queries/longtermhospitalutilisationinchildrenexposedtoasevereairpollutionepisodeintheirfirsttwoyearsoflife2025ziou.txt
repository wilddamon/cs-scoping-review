"Background: There are limited studies of children's long-term health impacts from landscape fire smoke emissions, despite the increasing threat of these events. This study investigates long-term hospital utilisation following the Hazelwood mine fire (Feb-March 2014), in a cohort of children exposed during infancy. Method(s): Birth records of all children born in the Latrobe Valley, Australia, and aged under two during the fire (n = 1788), were linked to emergency department (ED) presentations and hospital admissions for a five-year period immediately following the event. Associations between modelled terciles of fire-specific particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter <=2.5 mum (PM2.5) and presentations/admissions, for all causes as well as for specific diagnostic groupings (respiratory, infectious, allergic), were assessed using a recurrent events survival analysis approach. Result(s): Children with medium (HR 1.16, 95 %CI 1.06-1.28) or high exposure (HR 1.11, 95 %CI 1.01-1.21) had higher rates of overall presentations to the ED than those with low exposure. Children with medium exposure still had marginally higher rates of presentation to the ED, after exclusion of diagnoses unlikely to have a clinical connection to smoke (HR 1.13, 95 %CI 1.00-1.27). Conclusion(s): We observed an increase during a five-year period in overall ED presentations in children with higher exposure to smoke during infancy, although these results were attenuated after adjusting for the distance between the residential area and the hospital.
