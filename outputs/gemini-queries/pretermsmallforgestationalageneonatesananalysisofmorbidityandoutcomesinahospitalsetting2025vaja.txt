"Background: Small for gestational age (SGA) neonates, defined as having birth weights below the 10th percentile for gestational age, face increased morbidity and mortality, especially when born preterm. This study evaluates neonatal morbidities and outcomes in preterm SGA neonates admitted to a tertiary care NICU. Method(s): A prospective observational study was conducted at Dhiraj Hospital over 1.5 years, including 110 preterm SGA neonates (<37 weeks). Data on maternal parameters, neonatal characteristics, and morbidities were collected and analyzed using SPSS. Result(s): In this study of 110 preterm SGA neonates, significant associations were observed between lower birth weight and increased neonatal morbidities. Infants weighing <1.5 kg had higher incidences of hypothermia (p=0.001), RDS (p=0.003), sepsis (p=0.002), IVH (p=0.04), and birth asphyxia (p=0.006), with overall mortality also significantly greater in this group (p=0.01). Similarly, neonates born before 32 weeks of gestation had increased resuscitation needs (p=0.002), longer hospital stays (p=0.005), and higher rates of jaundice (p=0.03) and mortality (p=0.004). Maternal factors such as anemia (p=0.008), PIH (p=0.01), and poor nutrition (p=0.02) were significantly associated with the occurrence of preterm SGA births. Inadequate antenatal care (p=0.007) and lack of antenatal corticosteroid administration (p=0.003) were also linked to adverse neonatal outcomes. While the mode of delivery showed no significant impact (p=0.21), anthropometric parameters like admission weight (p=0.001), head circumference (p=0.01), and length (p=0.02) varied significantly across birth weight and gestational age categories. These findings underscore the need for targeted maternal and neonatal care strategies to improve outcomes in this vulnerable group. Conclusion(s): This study highlights the high vulnerability of preterm SGA neonates, revealing that lower birth weight and gestational age are significantly linked to increased neonatal morbidities and mortality. Maternal factors like anemia, PIH, poor nutrition, and inadequate antenatal care further compound risks. Key anthropometric measures reflected varying health outcomes.
