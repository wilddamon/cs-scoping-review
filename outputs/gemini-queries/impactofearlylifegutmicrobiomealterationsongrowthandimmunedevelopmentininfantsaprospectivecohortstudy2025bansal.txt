"Background: The establishment of the gut microbiome during the first year of life is a critical determinant of immune maturation and somatic growth. Perinatal antibiotic exposure is one of the most common causes of early microbiome disruption in high-income settings. Objective(s): To quantify the longitudinal impact of early-life antibiotic-induced microbiome alterations on linear growth, weight gain, and immune-related outcomes in a prospective birth cohort. Method(s): In this prospective cohort study, 180 term infants were enrolled at birth and followed until 12 months. Systemic antibiotic exposure in the first 90 days was recorded. Stool samples were collected at 7 days, 3 months, and 6 months for 16S rRNA gene sequencing (V4 region, IlluminaNovaSeq). Alpha diversity (Shannon index), Bifidobacterium relative abundance, and dysbiosis index were primary microbiome exposures. Primary outcomes were length-for-age z-score (LAZ), weight-for-age z-score (WAZ), and incidence of physician-diagnosed infections and atopic disease at 12 months. Multivariable linear and logistic regression models adjusted for mode of delivery, breastfeeding duration, maternal BMI, and household smoke exposure. Result(s): Fifty-two infants (28.9%) received systemic antibiotics within the first 90 days. At 3 months, antibiotic-exposed infants exhibited significantly lower Shannon diversity (2.81 +/- 0.91 vs. 3.94 +/- 0.72, p < 0.001) and Bifidobacterium abundance (14.8 +/- 11.9% vs. 34.6 +/- 14.3%, p < 0.001). At 12 months, antibiotic exposure was independently associated with a -0.61 SD lower LAZ (95% CI -0.89 to -0.33, p < 0.001), a 42% higher odds of recurrent respiratory tract infections (OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.18-1.71, p = 0.004), and a 2.1-fold higher odds of eczema (OR 2.10, 95% CI 1.34-3.29, p = 0.001). Each 1-unit decrease in 3-month Shannon diversity was associated with a -0.38 SD reduction in 12-month LAZ (p < 0.001). Conclusion(s): Early-life antibiotic exposure induces persistent microbiome alterations that are independently associated with impaired linear growth and increased immune-mediated disease in the first year of life.
