"""abstract ID: apgarscoreandriskofneonataldeathamongpreterminfants2020cnattingius
"Preterm birth, defined as birth <=36 weeks' gestational age (GA), is the main cause of neonatal death, although numerous other factors can affect this risk. For example, studies have shown that very preterm infants (infants born at <=31 weeks' gestation) have a lower risk of neonatal death if they have a normal birth weight, are female, receive antenatal corticosteroids, and have a high 5-minute Apgar score. Apgar scores are measured in 5 categories: heart rate, respiratory effort, muscle tone, reflex irritability, and color. Each category is given a score of 0 to 2, with higher scores meaning a better neonatal condition. Lower Apgar scores, typically less than 4 or less than 7 at 1 and 5 minutes, are associated with an increased risk of neonatal death among term infants. Lower Apgar scores are more common among preterm infants in part because they are commonly associated with immature breathing drive and lower muscle tone. The purpose of this study was to assess the association between Apgar scores and neonatal death in preterm infants. The study included 113,300 nonmalformed, live-born premature infants from 22 weeks 0 days' to 36 weeks 6 days' gestation from the Swedish Medical Birth Register from January 1, 1992, to December 31, 2016. Infant Apgar scores were measured at 1, 5, and 10 minutes after delivery, and neonatal death was defined as occurring within the first 27 days of life per the Cause of Death Register. Analyses were stratified according to GA. Adjusted relative risks of neonatal death and absolute rate differences in neonatal mortality were estimated according to the Apgar scores at 5 and 10 minutes. There was a 1.8% neonatal death rate among the 113,300 nonmalformed preterm infants included in this study. As GA decreased, neonatal death increased. At 36 weeks, there was a death rate of 0.2%, whereas at 22 weeks, there was a death rate of 76.5%. Furthermore, neonatal mortality increased as Apgar scores at 5 and 10 minutes decreased. Low Apgar scores (defined as 0-3 or 4-6) were more prevalent among infants of mothers who had placental abruption or hypertensive disease or infants who had low birth weight for GA or were delivered by cesarean. Furthermore, low Apgar scores (0 or 1, 2 or 3, and 4-6) at 5 and 10 minutes were more common for very preterm infants between 22 and 24 weeks' gestation. Infants born at 22 to 24 weeks with an Apgar score of 0 to 1 had 5 times the relative risk of neonatal mortality compared with those with an Apgar score of 9 to 10. For infants born 35 to 36 weeks, the relative risk was more than 300 times higher. In terms of the absolute rate difference, neonatal mortality increased substantially with decreasing Apgar scores in all gestational strata compared with infants with a 5-minute Apgar score of 9 or 10. The relative risks of neonatal death according to Apgar score were also consistent with those in the cohort for analyses stratified according to year of birth and GA. Overall, Apgar scores provided prognostic information regarding the risk of neonatal death among preterm infants. The relative risk of neonatal death was consistent in the cohort in that the rate of neonatal death increased as Apgar score decreased. Similarly, the absolute rate differences also increased as Apgar scores decreased. Furthermore, increasing Apgar scores from 5 to 10 minutes were associated with a lower neonatal mortality rate compared with those that stayed stable. This was true even for reference-range Apgar scores (ie, 7-8 or 9-10). These findings support the idea that Apgar scores are a useful tool in assessing the health of preterm infants.Copyright (c) 2020 Wolters Kluwer Health, Inc. All rights reserved."""
