"OBJECTIVE: The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) advises the administration of RSVpreF (Abrysvo), the only authorized bivalent prefusion F respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccine for pregnant women, between 32 and 36 weeks of gestation,1 aiming to maximize neonatal immunity during the RSV season (September to January). However, real-world data on postmarketing vaccine uptake remain limited. We aimed to investigate postvaccination maternal outcomes using real-word data and monitor RSV vaccine uptake changes in the first season in association with other maternal vaccines. STUDY DESIGN: This study queried the TrinetX U.S. Collaborative cohort, a real-time federated health research network with electronic health records of around 120 million individuals. We defined the pregnancy cohort by identifying deliveries2 from September 1st, 2023, to April 8th, 2024 (10 weeks after the last date of maternal RSV vaccine recommendation: January 31st, 2024). The geographic distribution of this cohort is 22% Northeast, 15% Midwest, 53% South, and 10% West. The index date was defined as the vaccination date for the RSVpreF group and as 32 to 36 weeks of gestation for the control group; 9.3% received RSVpreF in the cohort. The RSVpreF group patients were more likely to be older, vaccinated with other prenatal vaccines, utilize preventative care, use assisted reproductive technologies, aspirin in pregnancy, and less likely to be Hispanic or Black (Supplemental Table 1) compared to the control group before propensity score matching. We performed 1:1 propensity score matching based on maternal age, demographics, health utilization, comorbidities, medications, assisted reproductive technology, multigestation, multiparity, history of pregnancy complications, social factors, and body mass index. Maternal and limited neonatal outcomes (small and large for gestational age) were identified by International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision codes (Supplemental Table 2) and followed-up with a half-year period after the index date. We also delineated the monthly trend in maternal RSVpreF, COVID-19, influenza, and Tdap (tetanus, diphtheria, and acellular pertussis) vaccinations between this period. The Chung Shan Medical University Hospital's Institutional review board approved the use of TriNetX for this research (CSMUH No: CS2- 21176). RESULTS: Risk ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for outcomes were estimated between 6387 vaccinated and nonvaccinated women. There were no increased risks of preterm birth, gestational hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes, placental abruption, hemorrhage (ante-, intra-, and postpartum), preterm premature rupture of membranes, chorioamnionitis, deep vein thrombosis, maternal cardiovascular, or neonatal outcomes associated with the maternal RSV vaccination compared to the nonvaccinated cohort (Figure 1e1). The vaccinated group had a slightly lower risk of cesarean delivery (risk ratio, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.86e0.96) and oligohydramnios (risk ratio, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.61e0.95) after propensity score matching matching (Supplemental Table 3). RSV vaccination rates rose from September 2023 and peaked in January 2024. Other vaccinations (COVID- 19, influenza, and Tdap) followed seasonal trends (Figure 1). Although there was limited vaccination brand data, RSVpreF3 (Arexvy), which is not recommended for the pregnancy population was administered 19 times over 7 months, and new RSV vaccines were administered post-1/ 31/2024, mandating ongoing surveillance. The national RSV vaccination coverage by CDC was 17.8%, compared to 9.3% in our cohort, indicating potential for higher uptake. CONCLUSION: Despite an imbalance in preterm birth in the Maternal Immunization Study for Safety and Efficacy phase 3 trial,3 this multisite U.S. study suggests that maternal RSV vaccine at 32 to 36 weeks of gestation not associated with an increase in preterm birth. No preterm birth signal was detected in the CDC Vaccine Safety Datalink,4 which samples different regions from TriNetX. There was no increased risk of gestational hypertensive disorders, contrasting with smaller study by Son et al5 in New York. However, this analysis is limited by the absence of individual patient-level or linked maternal-neonatal data and lack of granularity for gestational age at vaccination or birth. This analysis does not establish causation, as residual confounding may still be present. RSV vaccination rate rose gradually through the RSV season, peaking in January, before declining, likely as awareness increased during the RSV season.
