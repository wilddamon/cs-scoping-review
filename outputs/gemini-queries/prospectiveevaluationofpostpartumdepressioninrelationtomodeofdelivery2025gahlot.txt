"Background: Postpartum depression (PPD) is a common but often underdiagnosed mental health condition affecting women during the postnatal period. It can significantly impact maternal well-being, infant bonding, and family health. Various biological, obstetric, and psychosocial factors contribute to its development, among which mode of delivery has gained growing attention. Understanding how cesarean and vaginal deliveries influence the risk of PPD is essential for early identification and intervention in high-risk mothers. Aim(s): The present study aimed to prospectively evaluate the incidence and severity of postpartum depression in relation to the mode of delivery and to identify associated demographic, obstetric, and psychosocial risk factors among postpartum women in a tertiary care hospital. Material(s) and Method(s): A prospective observational study was conducted in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at a tertiary care hospital on 84 postpartum women aged 18-40 years. Data on sociodemographic factors, parity, antenatal complications, and mode of delivery were collected using a predesigned proforma. Postpartum depression was assessed between the third and seventh postpartum day using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), with a score >=13 indicating probable depression. Result(s): The prevalence of postpartum depression was 26.19%. Depression was significantly higher among women who underwent cesarean section (36.84%) compared to those with vaginal delivery (17.39%) (p = 0.048). Antenatal complications (45.45%, p = 0.021) and inadequate social support (p = 0.002) were also significantly associated with PPD. No significant association was observed with age, parity, or type of family. Conclusion(s): Postpartum depression remains a considerable public health concern, affecting more than one-fourth of postpartum women. Cesarean delivery, antenatal complications, and poor social support were key determinants.
